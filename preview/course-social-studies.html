What Social Studies builds, K → 12

Learners begin close to home — family, neighborhood, maps, and community helpers — then widen the circle outward: the United States and its regions, the great world civilizations, and finally the systems that govern nations and economies. Along the way students practice reading maps, weighing evidence, telling cause from effect, comparing sources, and forming reasoned arguments. History is taught honestly and globally: every people and faith is presented respectfully and accurately, including the Islamic Golden Age alongside Greece, Rome, and the wider medieval world.

Sample lesson · Unit 2

Reading a Map of My Bedroom

Objective: Understand that a map shows a place from above and uses symbols, then make a simple map with a key.

Imagine you are a bird flying over your room, looking straight down. Your bed becomes a rectangle, your rug a circle, your door a small line. That is what a map does — it draws places from above and uses little pictures called symbols. A box that tells what each symbol means is called a map key.

Practice: Draw your bedroom or kitchen from above on paper. Use a square for a table, a rectangle for the bed, a triangle for a chair. Make a key at the bottom that explains each symbol. Then use words like next to, above, and below to tell a grown-up where three things are.
Sample lesson · Unit 3

Needs, Wants & Saying Thank You

Objective: Sort items into needs and wants and explain why we save and give thanks for what we have.

A need is something we must have to live and be safe — food, clean water, a home, clothes, and people who love us. A want is something nice to have but that we can live without, like a new toy or candy. Knowing the difference helps us make good choices and remember to be grateful to God for what He provides.

Practice: Look at picture cards (apple, teddy bear, water, video game, jacket, ice cream, house, sticker). Put each into a Needs pile or a Wants pile. Then name one need you are thankful for today and one way you can share with someone who has less.

Sample lesson · Unit 1

The Five Regions of the United States

Objective: Locate and describe the five U.S. regions and explain how geography shapes how people live and work in each.

Geographers group the 50 states into five regions that share land and climate: the Northeast (crowded cities and cold winters), the Southeast (warm, humid, rivers and farmland), the Midwest — America's "breadbasket" of flat plains and wheat and corn — the Southwest (dry deserts and canyons), and the West (mountains, forests, and the Pacific coast). Where people live shapes their jobs: fishing on the coasts, farming on the plains, mining and ranching in the mountains and deserts.

Practice: On a blank U.S. map, color each region a different color and add its name. Pick one region and list two landforms, one crop or industry, and one big city there. Write a sentence: "In the ___ region, geography means people often work as ___."
Sample lesson · Unit 5

Why Did the Colonists Rebel?

Objective: Explain the main causes of the American Revolution and connect a cause to its effect.

After costly wars, Britain taxed the colonies on paper, tea, and other goods — but the colonists had no representatives in Parliament to vote on those taxes. Their cry became "No taxation without representation." Anger grew after events like the Boston Massacre and the Boston Tea Party of 1773, when colonists dumped British tea into the harbor. In 1776, leaders signed the Declaration of Independence, stating that people have rights and that governments should serve the governed.

Practice: Make a two-column cause-and-effect chart. In the left column write three British actions (Stamp Act, tea tax, closing Boston's port). In the right column write how colonists responded to each. Then answer in one sentence: what did colonists mean by "no taxation without representation"?

Sample lesson · Unit 2

Hammurabi's Code: Written Law in Babylon

Objective: Analyze how one of history's earliest written law codes reveals the values and social order of ancient Mesopotamia.

Around 1754 BCE, King Hammurabi of Babylon had 282 laws carved onto a tall stone pillar so that everyone could see them. This was one of the first times a society wrote down its laws in public — an important step, because now rulers and ordinary people could be judged by the same known rules. The code covered trade, family, farming, and crime, often using the principle of "an eye for an eye." Yet punishments differed by social class, showing that Babylon was not an equal society.

Practice: Read three sample laws from the code. For each, identify (1) what problem it addresses and (2) what it tells us Babylonians valued. Then write a short paragraph: Why was writing down the law such an important idea, even if the punishments seem harsh to us today?
Sample lesson · Unit 6

The House of Wisdom & the Islamic Golden Age

Objective: Explain how scholars of the medieval Islamic world advanced knowledge and connected the learning of many cultures.

From roughly the 8th to the 13th centuries, cities like Baghdad, Córdoba, and Cairo became centers of learning. In Baghdad's House of Wisdom, scholars translated Greek, Persian, and Indian texts into Arabic and then built on them. The mathematician al-Khwarizmi developed algebra (the word comes from Arabic al-jabr) and helped spread the number system we still use. Ibn al-Haytham pioneered the study of optics and the scientific method, while Ibn Sina (Avicenna) wrote a medical encyclopedia used for centuries. This knowledge later flowed into Europe, helping spark the Renaissance — a reminder that human progress is shared across cultures.

Practice: Create a "scholar trading card" for one figure (al-Khwarizmi, Ibn Sina, Ibn al-Haytham, or Fatima al-Fihri, who founded one of the world's oldest universities). Include their field, one contribution, and one way that contribution still affects life today. Then explain in a sentence how translation helped ideas travel between civilizations.

Sample lesson · Government & Civics · Unit 3

Separation of Powers & Checks and Balances

Objective: Explain why the framers divided federal power into three branches and analyze how each branch can check the others.

Fearing that concentrated power leads to tyranny, the framers of the Constitution split the federal government into three branches: the legislative (Congress) makes laws, the executive (the President) carries them out, and the judicial (the courts) interprets them. To keep any one branch from dominating, each is given powers to check the others — for example, Congress passes a bill, but the President can veto it; Congress can override that veto with a two-thirds vote; and the Supreme Court can rule a law unconstitutional. This system deliberately makes government slower but harder to abuse.

Practice: Draw the three-branch triangle. On each connecting arrow, label one specific check (e.g., "Senate confirms judges," "President vetoes bills," "Court reviews laws"). Then write a paragraph responding to the prompt: Do checks and balances protect freedom, or do they make government too slow to act? Support your position with one historical example.
Sample lesson · Economics · Unit 2

How Supply and Demand Set a Price

Objective: Use supply and demand to explain how markets determine prices and predict the effect of a shift in either.

In a market, demand shows how much buyers will purchase at each price (usually more when prices are low), and supply shows how much sellers will offer (usually more when prices are high). Where the two curves cross is the equilibrium price — the price at which the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer. When something changes — a drought cuts the coffee harvest, or a new trend boosts demand for a phone — the curves shift and the price moves to a new equilibrium.

Practice: Sketch supply and demand curves for concert tickets and mark the equilibrium. Then predict what happens to price and quantity in each case: (1) the band suddenly becomes hugely popular, (2) the venue doubles the number of seats. Explain which curve shifted and why, and connect it to something you have actually bought.
Materials & assessments

What a learner does

  • Lessons & readings — short illustrated lessons, primary-source excerpts, maps, and timelines appropriate to each band.
  • Practice & skills — map work, vocabulary, cause-and-effect charts, and document analysis.
  • Quizzes & checks — auto-graded checks for understanding after each unit (see Learn).
  • Projects & DBQs — timelines, region posters, "scholar trading cards," and document-based writing in the upper grades.
  • Discussion & narration — oral narration in K–5, seminar-style discussion and structured essays in 6–12.

How progress is measured

  • K–2 — standards-based (developing / on track / secure) via observation and simple tasks.
  • 3–5 — quizzes, projects, and map assessments with letter or percentage marks.
  • 6–8 — unit tests, source analyses, and a research project each term.
  • 9–12 — exams, document-based essays, and a capstone; each course earns high-school credit toward the transcript.
  • Portfolio-ready — work samples export cleanly for Pennsylvania's annual portfolio and evaluator review.
