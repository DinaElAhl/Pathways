What this subject builds, K → 12

Math at Pathways is designed as a single, coherent staircase. Early grades build number sense — counting, cardinality, and place value — the foundation everything else stands on. The elementary years turn those numbers into fluent operations and open the door to fractions and decimals. Middle school shifts from computing answers to reasoning about relationships: ratios, proportions, negative numbers, and the first true algebraic thinking. High school formalizes that reasoning into Algebra, Geometry, and beyond, ending with Pre-Calculus and Statistics that prepare learners for college math and everyday quantitative life.

Each course spirals: skills are introduced, practiced to fluency, then revisited at greater depth in later grades — so a fraction learned in 4th grade becomes a rational expression in Algebra II.

Standards alignment

Units are written to Common Core-style objectives — the same scope and sequence families recognize from state math standards (counting & cardinality, operations & algebraic thinking, number & operations, ratios & proportional relationships, expressions & equations, functions, geometry, statistics & probability). We describe our alignment to these widely-used objectives; Pathways is an independent curriculum and does not claim official endorsement.

Precision & stewardship. Math rewards care: checking your work, measuring honestly, and being patient with a hard problem. We treat accuracy and diligence as quiet habits worth building — a fair measure and an honest answer are small acts of good character.

Making Ten to Add

Objective: Add two single-digit numbers by first making a group of ten.

When we add numbers like 8 + 5, it helps to make a friendly ten first. Ten is easy to add to. We ask: "How many does 8 need to reach 10?" It needs 2. So we borrow 2 from the 5, which leaves 3. Now the problem becomes 10 + 3 = 13. We didn't change the total — we just rearranged it into a shape our brains like.

Practice activity
Use ten-frames and counters. Fill the first frame to make 10, then count the leftovers. Solve: 9 + 4, 7 + 6, 8 + 7. Say each one aloud: "8 plus 7 — 8 needs 2 to make ten, 5 left over, so 10 and 5 is 15."

Counting Coins to Buy a Snack

Objective: Count a mixed set of coins and compare it to a price.

Each coin has its own value: a penny is 1¢, a nickel is 5¢, a dime is 10¢, and a quarter is 25¢. To count a handful of coins, we start with the biggest values and add on. If we have a quarter, two dimes, and a nickel, we count: 25… 35… 45… 50. That's 50¢ — enough for a 45¢ apple, with a nickel to spare.

Practice activity
Set out play coins. Count these three purses: (a) 3 dimes + 1 nickel, (b) 1 quarter + 4 pennies, (c) 2 quarters + 2 dimes. Which purse can buy a 60¢ juice box? Handling money carefully — counting twice before you pay — is a good habit to keep.

Equivalent Fractions with a Number Line

Objective: Show why two fractions that look different can name the same amount.

A fraction is a number, and every number has a spot on the number line. If we draw a line from 0 to 1 and split it into 2 equal parts, the mark in the middle is 1/2. Now split the same line into 4 equal parts — the second mark lands in exactly the same place: 2/4. Same spot, same value. We can make equivalents by multiplying the top and bottom by the same number: 1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6, because we're just cutting each piece into more, smaller pieces.

Practice activity
Fold a strip of paper in halves, then fourths, then eighths. Mark where 1/2, 2/4, and 4/8 land — they should line up. Then find two fractions equivalent to 2/3 and prove it by multiplying top and bottom by the same number.

Finding Volume with Unit Cubes

Objective: Measure the volume of a rectangular prism by counting cubes and by using a formula.

Volume is how much space is inside a solid — how many unit cubes fit. If a box is 4 cubes long, 3 cubes wide, and 2 cubes tall, we could count all 24 cubes one at a time. But there's a shortcut: one layer has 4 × 3 = 12 cubes, and there are 2 layers, so 12 × 2 = 24. That's why the formula is V = l × w × h. Volume is always measured in cubic units.

Practice activity
Build a 5 × 2 × 3 prism with blocks (or draw it). Predict the volume with the formula, then count to check. Find the volume of a box that is 6 cm × 4 cm × 5 cm. Careful measuring pays off — a small mistake in one edge changes the whole answer.

Understanding Unit Rate

Objective: Find and use a unit rate to compare and make decisions.

A rate compares two different quantities — like miles and hours, or dollars and ounces. A unit rate tells us the amount for exactly one of something, which makes comparisons fair. If a 12-oz box of cereal costs $3.60 and an 18-oz box costs $4.86, which is the better deal? Divide to get price per ounce: 3.60 ÷ 12 = $0.30/oz and 4.86 ÷ 18 = $0.27/oz. The bigger box is cheaper per ounce. The unit rate cut through the confusion.

Practice activity
Find the unit rate: (a) 150 miles in 3 hours, (b) $5.00 for 4 apples, (c) 240 words typed in 6 minutes. Then decide: is 8 notebooks for $10 or 5 notebooks for $6 the better buy? Show the per-item price for each. An honest comparison — checking the real per-unit cost — is good stewardship of what you have.

Solving Two-Step Equations

Objective: Solve equations of the form ax + b = c by undoing operations in order.

An equation is a balance: whatever we do to one side, we must do to the other. To solve 3x + 5 = 20, we undo the operations in reverse order. First subtract 5 from both sides: 3x = 15. Then divide both sides by 3: x = 5. We can check by substituting back: 3(5) + 5 = 20. ✓ The check is not optional — it's how we know we're right.

Practice activity
Solve and check each: 2x − 7 = 9, 4x + 3 = 27, (x/2) + 6 = 10. Then write an equation for this story and solve it: "A gym charges a $10 sign-up fee plus $4 per visit. How many visits cost $34?"

Solving Quadratics with the Quadratic Formula (Algebra I)

Objective: Solve any quadratic equation using the quadratic formula.

Not every quadratic factors nicely, so we need a tool that always works. For any equation in the form ax² + bx + c = 0, the solutions are x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / (2a). Take x² + 3x − 10 = 0: here a = 1, b = 3, c = −10. The discriminant is b² − 4ac = 9 + 40 = 49, and √49 = 7. So x = (−3 ± 7) / 2, giving x = 2 or x = −5. The ± sign is why a quadratic can have two solutions.

Practice activity
Solve with the formula: x² − 5x + 6 = 0, 2x² + 4x − 6 = 0, x² + 2x − 5 = 0 (leave in radical form). For each, compute the discriminant first and predict how many real solutions there are.

The Normal Distribution & the Empirical Rule (Statistics)

Objective: Use the 68–95–99.7 rule to reason about normally distributed data.

Many real measurements — heights, test scores, measurement errors — pile up in a symmetric, bell-shaped curve. For any normal distribution, about 68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within 2, and about 99.7% within 3. If adult heights have a mean of 170 cm and a standard deviation of 8 cm, then roughly 95% of people are between 170 − 16 = 154 cm and 170 + 16 = 186 cm. The rule turns a vague "most people" into a defensible estimate.

Practice activity
A test has mean 500 and standard deviation 100. What score range holds the middle 68%? The middle 95%? Roughly what percent of scores are above 700? Sketch the curve and label the standard-deviation marks before answering.
