Sample lesson · Middle 6–8

Islamic Geometric Patterns: The Six-Fold Rosette

Objective
Construct a six-fold geometric star pattern using only a compass and straightedge, and explain how repetition and symmetry create the endless "infinite" designs found in mosques, tilework, and manuscripts.
Lesson
Islamic artists turned to geometry and arabesque (flowing plant forms) as a beautiful, boundless visual language. Every complex pattern begins with a simple circle. We divide a circle into six equal parts by "walking" the compass around its edge at the same radius — each mark becomes a petal. Connecting the points builds a hexagon and a six-pointed star; extending the lines creates an interlaced rosette that can tile forever with no beginning or end.
Practice activity
1) Draw a circle. 2) Keeping the compass width the same, step around the edge to make 6 marks. 3) Connect every point to form the star and hexagon. 4) Ink the pattern and add a two-color arabesque fill, then note one place you've seen a similar design. Exit check: Why does keeping the compass the same width guarantee the shape is symmetric?

Sample lesson · Elementary 3–5

Counting the Beat: Quarter and Eighth Notes

Objective
Read and clap a four-beat rhythm in 4/4 time, correctly performing quarter notes ("ta") and paired eighth notes ("ti-ti").
Lesson
Music is organized by a steady beat, like a heartbeat. In 4/4 time there are four beats in each measure. A quarter note gets one beat — we say "ta." Two eighth notes share one beat — we say "ti-ti," twice as fast. When we read left to right and keep the pulse even, the rhythm sings itself. Many songs we love, including simple nasheed, ride on exactly these patterns.
Practice activity
Clap and say this line while a steady beat plays: ta   ti-ti   ta   ta. Then try: ti-ti   ti-ti   ta   ta. Finally, compose your own 4-beat measure using two quarters and two pairs of eighths, and perform it. Exit check: How many eighth notes equal one quarter note?

Sample lesson · High School 9–12

First Aid: Responding to a Minor Wound

Objective
Demonstrate the correct steps to clean and dress a minor cut, and explain when an injury needs professional medical care.
Lesson
Caring for the body is a responsibility we all share. For a minor cut: first ensure the scene is safe and wash your own hands. Apply gentle pressure with a clean cloth to stop bleeding, then rinse the wound under clean running water. Pat dry, apply antiseptic if available, and cover with a sterile bandage. Watch for warning signs — deep or gaping wounds, bleeding that won't stop after 10 minutes of pressure, signs of infection (redness, warmth, pus), or a wound from a dirty or rusty object — all of which need a medical professional.
Practice activity
With a partner and a first-aid kit, role-play treating a scraped knee: talk through each step aloud in order, then bandage a (pretend) wound. Afterward, list three signs that would tell you to stop and seek professional help. Exit check: Why do you wash your own hands before touching someone else's wound?

Sample lesson · High School 9–12

Python: Your First Loop

Objective
Write a Python for loop that repeats an action a set number of times, and explain why loops save us from repeating ourselves.
Lesson
Computers are patient with repetition — that's a loop's job. A for loop runs a block of code once for each item in a range. In Python, range(5) counts 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. The indented line beneath the loop is what repeats:

for i in range(5):
    print("Bismillah — starting task", i)

This prints five numbered lines instead of us typing print five times. The variable i holds the current count, so we can even use it inside the loop.
Practice activity
Write a loop that prints the numbers 1 through 10, then modify it to print only the even numbers (hint: range(2, 11, 2)). As a challenge, use a loop to add the numbers 1–10 and print the total. Exit check: How many times does the body of for i in range(3): run, and what values does i take?

Sample lesson · Middle 6–8

Ser vs. Estar: Two Ways to "Be"

Objective
Choose correctly between ser and estar when describing people and situations, and conjugate each in the present tense.
Lesson
English has one verb "to be," but Spanish has two. Use ser for permanent, defining traits — identity, origin, personality: Yo soy estudiante (I am a student), Ella es amable (She is kind). Use estar for temporary states, feelings, and location: Estoy cansado (I am tired), El libro está en la mesa (The book is on the table). A helpful memory hook: ser = who you are, estar = how you are right now and where you are.
Practice activity
Fill each blank with the correct form of ser or estar: 1) Nosotros ___ hermanos. 2) María ___ en la escuela. 3) Yo ___ feliz hoy. 4) Tú ___ muy inteligente. Then write two sentences about yourself — one with ser, one with estar. Exit check: Which verb would you use to say "I am happy today," and why?

Sample lesson · High School 9–12

Building Your First Monthly Budget

Objective
Create a simple monthly budget that divides income into saving, spending, and giving, and explain why "pay yourself first" builds strong habits.
Lesson
A budget is just a plan for your money before you spend it. Start with your income — an allowance, a small job, or a gift. Then sort your money into categories. A helpful starting framework is 50 / 30 / 20: about 50% for needs, 30% for wants, and 20% for saving and giving. Wise practice is to "pay yourself first" — set aside savings (and sadaqah, charitable giving) at the top, before spending — so generosity and saving aren't just leftovers. Tracking every dollar keeps the plan honest.
Practice activity
Imagine a monthly income of $100. Build a budget table: list at least three categories (e.g., saving, giving, spending, a goal you're saving toward) with a dollar amount each, and make sure the amounts total $100. Then write one sentence naming something you'd save toward and why. Exit check: If you earned $80 this month, how much would go to saving under the 20% rule?
