Science

Observe carefully, ask good questions, test ideas — and marvel at an ordered, knowable world.

← All subjects
K–12 · Standards-aligned · Hands-on labs

A curiosity-first path from the five senses to the AP-level lab bench

Pathways Academy Science grows a learner from noticing the world with their own senses to reasoning about cells, atoms, forces, and galaxies. Every band centers on the practices real scientists use — observe, question, predict, test, measure, and explain with evidence — so children build habits of mind, not just facts to memorize. Units are sequenced so early wonder (plants, weather, magnets) becomes middle-grade models (cells, atoms, energy) and finally the discipline of high-school Biology, Chemistry, and Physics.

Nature & Senses→ Earth & Life Science→ Physical Science & Chemistry Basics→ Biology · Chemistry · Physics
A sense of wonder, woven in gently. Studying creation is treated as an invitation to attentiveness and gratitude — the more closely we look at a leaf's veins, a snowflake's symmetry, or the tilt that gives us seasons, the more there is to appreciate. Faith framing stays light and never replaces the science: learners still test, measure, and explain with evidence, and are encouraged to hold questions open with humility.

Standards & approach

Units are written to mirror NGSS-style three-dimensional learning — pairing disciplinary core ideas (life, physical, earth & space science) with science & engineering practices and crosscutting concepts such as patterns, cause & effect, systems, energy & matter, and structure & function. Objectives are aligned to these frameworks for planning and portfolio purposes; Pathways Academy is an independent curriculum and is not affiliated with or endorsed by NGSS, Achieve, or any state board. High-school courses are structured to satisfy typical lab-science graduation and college-admission expectations.

Sample lessons

Living or Non-Living? A Sorting Hunt

Unit 2~20 minGrades K–1
Objective

Sort objects into living and non-living and explain the sort using at least one need of living things (food, water, air, or growth).

Lesson

Living things do three special jobs: they grow, they need food and water, and they can make more of themselves (a cat has kittens; a maple drops seeds). A rock, a spoon, and a toy never do any of those — they are non-living. Some things, like a fallen leaf or a wooden chair, were once living but are not alive now. We look closely and ask: "Does it eat? Does it grow? Could it have babies or seeds?"

Practice activity

Take a basket on a 10-minute walk around the house or yard and collect 6 small safe items (a leaf, a pebble, a flower, a bottle cap…). Make two hoops on the floor labeled Living and Non-Living. Place each item and say one reason aloud. Bonus: start a third hoop for Once-Living. Draw your favorite living thing and write what it needs.

Lab: Will It Sink or Float?

Hands-on LabUnit 6Grades K–2
Objective

Predict and test whether everyday objects sink or float, then look for a pattern in the results.

Materials

A clear tub or sink of water, a towel, and 8 safe objects made of different materials: cork, spoon, crayon, coin, plastic lid, small rock, sponge, apple.

Do this
  1. Before testing each object, predict: point thumbs-up (float) or thumbs-down (sink).
  2. Gently place the object on the water. Watch what happens.
  3. Record on a two-column chart: Float / Sink (draw or place the item).
  4. After all 8, look at your chart. What do the floaters have in common? The sinkers?
Talk about it

Which predictions were right? Were any surprising? (An apple floats even though it feels heavy!) Scientists change their minds when the water shows them something new — and that's a wonderful thing.

Following the Water Cycle

Unit 5~35 minGrade 4
Objective

Describe the four main stages of the water cycle and explain that the sun's energy drives it.

Lesson

Water is always on the move. The sun heats oceans, lakes, and puddles, turning liquid water into invisible vapor — evaporation. High in the cooler sky, vapor turns back into tiny droplets — condensation — that gather into clouds. When droplets grow heavy, they fall as rain, snow, sleet, or hail — precipitation. That water flows into streams and rivers, or soaks into the ground — collection — and the cycle begins again. Not one drop is wasted; the same water has cycled through the Earth for ages.

Practice activity

Draw and label the cycle in a circle with four arrows. Then write a "day in the life of a water droplet" story that visits all four stages at least once (start in the ocean, ride a cloud, fall as snow on a mountain, melt into a river). Underline each stage word in your story.

Lab: Build a Water Cycle in a Bag

Hands-on LabUnit 5Grades 3–5
Objective

Model evaporation, condensation, and precipitation in a sealed bag and connect the model to the real water cycle.

Materials

A zip-top sandwich bag, permanent marker, 3 tablespoons of water, a few drops of blue food coloring, tape, and a sunny window.

Do this
  1. With the marker, draw a sun, a cloud, and waves near the bottom of the bag.
  2. Pour in the blue water, press out the air, and seal tightly.
  3. Tape the bag to a sunny window and observe over the day.
  4. Record what you see at 1 hour, 3 hours, and the next morning.
What's happening

The sun warms the water so it evaporates and rises as vapor. It condenses into droplets on the cool top of the bag, and when droplets get heavy they slide down like precipitation. Write a sentence matching each thing you saw to its water-cycle word.

Extend

Fair-test question: does a bag in the shade cycle as fast as one in the sun? Set up both and compare — what does that tell you about the sun's role?

Predicting Traits with Punnett Squares

Unit 6~45 minGrade 7–8
Objective

Use a Punnett square to predict the probability of offspring traits for a single gene with dominant and recessive alleles.

Lesson

Genes come in versions called alleles. A capital letter marks the dominant allele, a lowercase letter the recessive. If a pea plant has even one dominant allele for purple flowers (P), it shows purple; only pp shows white. Cross two Pp parents and set up a 2×2 grid with each parent's alleles along the sides. Fill each box by combining the row and column allele: you get PP, Pp, Pp, pp. That's a 3:1 ratio — a 75% chance of purple, 25% chance of white. The square predicts probability, like flipping a coin, not a guarantee for any single seed.

Practice activity

Work these crosses and give the phenotype ratio: (a) Tt × tt (tall is dominant), (b) Bb × Bb (brown eyes dominant), (c) RR × rr. For each, state the percent chance of the recessive trait appearing. Then explain in one sentence why two brown-eyed parents can still have a blue-eyed child.

Lab: Conservation of Mass in a Reaction

Hands-on LabUnit 3Grade 7
Objective

Show that mass is conserved in a chemical reaction by massing the reactants and products in a closed system.

Materials

A zip-top bag, baking soda (1 tsp), white vinegar (30 mL) in a small open cup or balloon, a kitchen scale (0.1 g), and safety goggles.

Do this
  1. Place the baking soda in the bag and the cup of vinegar upright inside the bag without spilling it. Seal the bag with air squeezed out.
  2. Measure and record the total mass of the sealed bag and contents (this is your reactant mass).
  3. Without opening, tip the bag so the vinegar mixes with the baking soda. Observe the fizzing and the bag inflating.
  4. After the reaction stops, mass the sealed bag again (product mass). Record.
Analyze

Compare before and after masses — they should match within measurement error, even though a gas (carbon dioxide) formed. Atoms were only rearranged, not created or destroyed. Predict: what would the scale show if you opened the bag and let the gas escape, then re-massed? Test it and explain.

Safety

Wear goggles. The reaction is safe but the bag can inflate firmly — do not over-fill.

Balancing Equations & the Mole (Chemistry, Unit 3)

Chemistry~50 minGrade 10
Objective

Balance a chemical equation and use mole ratios to calculate the mass of product formed from a given mass of reactant.

Lesson

A balanced equation obeys conservation of mass: the same number of each atom appears on both sides. Take the combustion of methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. The coefficients are mole ratios — 1 mole CH₄ reacts with 2 moles O₂. To go from grams to grams, use the mole map: grams → moles (÷ molar mass) → moles of the other substance (× mole ratio) → grams (× molar mass). Molar mass comes from the periodic table (CH₄ ≈ 16.0 g/mol; CO₂ ≈ 44.0 g/mol).

Worked example

How many grams of CO₂ form from 32.0 g of CH₄? 32.0 g ÷ 16.0 g/mol = 2.0 mol CH₄ → (1:1 ratio) → 2.0 mol CO₂ → × 44.0 g/mol = 88.0 g CO₂.

Practice

Balance and solve: (1) H₂ + O₂ → H₂O — how many grams of water from 8.0 g H₂? (2) 2Al + 3CuCl₂ → 2AlCl₃ + 3Cu — moles of Cu from 4.0 mol Al? Show the mole map for each.

Lab: Measuring the Acceleration of Gravity (Physics, Unit 1)

Hands-on LabPhysicsGrade 11
Objective

Experimentally determine g, the acceleration due to gravity, from the motion of a rolling ball or a falling object, and evaluate the result against the accepted 9.8 m/s².

Materials

A smooth ramp or long board, a marble or steel ball, a meter stick, painter's tape, and a phone stopwatch (or a free video-analysis app for slow-motion frames).

Procedure
  1. Set the ramp at a fixed low angle and mark the start line. Measure the ramp length and angle θ.
  2. Release the ball from rest and time how long it takes to travel measured distances (0.25 m, 0.50 m, 0.75 m, 1.00 m). Do 3 trials each and average.
  3. For each distance, use d = ½at² to solve for the ramp acceleration a = 2d / t².
  4. Correct for the incline: g = a / sin θ. (On a ramp the ball accelerates at g·sin θ.)
Analysis & write-up
  • Graph distance vs. time² — the slope equals ½a, a check on your calculation.
  • Compute percent error: |your g − 9.8| / 9.8 × 100%.
  • Identify two sources of error (reaction-time on the stopwatch, ramp friction) and state how each would push your value high or low.

Deliverable: a formal lab report — purpose, hypothesis, data table, calculations, graph, and a conclusion that discusses uncertainty.

The same simple ratio that balances an equation, and the same steady 9.8 m/s² that pulls a marble, hold everywhere we have ever measured. High-school science invites the awe of that reliability — a universe consistent enough to be studied, questioned, and understood.
