Faith Studies

Nurturing faith, character, and a lifelong connection to the Qur'an and the Arabic language — K through 12.
Faith Studies · a flagship program at Pathways Academy

Learn the language, love the Book, live the character.

Three connected strands grow together across every grade band — Arabic gives learners the key to the words, Qur'an builds recitation, memorization, and understanding, and Islamic Studies roots it all in belief, history, and beautiful character (akhlaq). Adab — good manners and mindfulness of Allah — is woven through every lesson.

How the program works

One program, three strands that grow together

Faith Studies is designed so the strands reinforce one another. In the early years a child hears and memorizes short surahs in Qur'an, learns the letters that spell them in Arabic, and meets the Prophet ﷺ and the prophets before him in Islamic Studies. As learners mature, Arabic reading unlocks direct engagement with the Qur'an, Tajweed refines their recitation, and Tafsir and Aqeedah help them understand and live what they read.

Each grade band builds on the last: Primary (K–2) centers wonder, sound, and story; Elementary (3–5) builds fluency and the Seerah; Middle (6–8) introduces grammar, Tajweed rules, and Fiqh; and High School (9–12) reaches comprehension of classical texts, Tafsir, Aqeedah, and contemporary ethics. Objectives are written as clear, measurable skills so families can track mastery and keep records for their state.

Adab & character, woven throughout

  • Every lesson opens with intention (niyyah) and the basmala.
  • Respect for the mushaf, teachers, parents, and classmates is modeled and practiced.
  • Du'as for daily life are learned alongside their meanings.
  • Character traits — honesty, gratitude, patience (sabr), kindness — are named, discussed, and tracked as habits.
  • The tone is warm and invitational, never fear-based; love of Allah and His Messenger ﷺ comes first.
A note on families & madhhab. Fiqh is taught with the core, agreed-upon practices first, and points of scholarly difference are presented respectfully. Families can align pronunciation, memorization pace, and jurisprudential detail to their own tradition in Settings — the curriculum is a flexible scaffold, not a replacement for a family's teacher or imam.

Sample lesson · Arabic I

Reading the family of baa: بَ بِ بُ

Primary K–2 · Unit 3 · ~20 minutes
Objective: Learners read the letter ب with each of the three short vowels and blend it with ت to read a first word.

We begin with the basmala. The teacher shows the bare letter ب (baa) and reminds learners it makes a "b" sound. Then a fatha appears above it — بَ — "ba, like the start of bāb." A kasra below gives بِ "bi," and a damma above gives بُ "bu." Learners echo each three times, watching where the little marks sit.

Practice activity — "Vowel hop"
  1. Point to بَ / بِ / بُ in random order; the child says the sound.
  2. Add the letter taa: read بَ + تَ and blend to say the word for "she stayed" — بَتَّ (keep it playful, focus on the blend).
  3. Write بَ, بِ, بُ once each, placing the vowel in the right spot.

Sample lesson · Arabic II

"Al-" and the sun & moon letters

Elementary 3–5 · Unit 3 · ~30 minutes
Objective: Learners add the definite article ال to a noun and read it correctly, distinguishing moon letters (where the "l" is pronounced) from sun letters (where it assimilates).

The article ال means "the." With moon letters we hear the "l": القَمَر is read al-qamar (the moon). With sun letters the "l" is silent and the next letter doubles: الشَّمس is read ash-shams (the sun), not al-shams. Learners feel the shadda that appears on sun letters.

Practice activity — "Sun or moon?"
  • Sort ten words into two columns by their first letter: sun (ت ث د ذ ر ز س ش ص ض ط ظ ل ن) or moon (the rest).
  • Read each with ال: البَاب (al-bāb), الرَّجُل (ar-rajul), الكِتَاب (al-kitāb), النُّور (an-nūr).
  • Write one sentence: "The house is big" — البَيتُ كَبِيرٌ.

Sample lesson · Arabic III

Conjugating the past tense: the pattern of فَعَلَ

Middle 6–8 · Unit 3 · ~40 minutes
Objective: Learners conjugate a sound three-letter verb in the past tense across the six most common pronouns and translate each form.

Arabic verbs grow from a root of (usually) three letters. The teaching verb فَعَلَ (fa-'a-la, "he did") is the model on which grammarians describe every pattern. In the past tense the base form فَعَلَ means "he did." We change the ending to change who did it: فَعَلَتْ (she did), فَعَلُوا (they did), فَعَلْتَ (you did, m.), فَعَلْتُ (I did), فَعَلْنَا (we did).

Practice activity — conjugation table
  1. Take the real root ك-ت-ب ("writing") and build the same six forms: كَتَبَ / كَتَبَتْ / كَتَبُوا / كَتَبْتَ / كَتَبْتُ / كَتَبْنَا.
  2. Translate each into English.
  3. Write one full sentence using a conjugated verb + subject, e.g. كَتَبَ الوَلَدُ الدَّرْسَ ("The boy wrote the lesson") and label the fi'l, fa'il, and object.

Sample lesson · Arabic IV

Reading Qur'anic Arabic: parsing a verse of Surah Al-'Asr

High School 9–12 · Unit 4 · ~45 minutes
Objective: Learners read وَالْعَصْرِ ۝ إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ لَفِي خُسْرٍ, identify each word's role, and explain how grammar carries the meaning.

Surah Al-'Asr is short but grammatically rich. وَالْعَصْرِ opens with the "waw of oath" (Allah swears by time), which puts العصر in the genitive (jarr) — hence the kasra ending. إِنَّ ("indeed") is one of the sisters of inna: it makes the following noun accusative, so الإنسان takes a fatha as its subject (ism inna). The لَـ in لَفِي is the "laam of emphasis," and فِي خُسْرٍ ("in loss") is a prepositional phrase serving as the predicate.

Practice activity — parse & render
  1. Label every word: oath particle, noun in jarr, emphatic particle, ism inna, laam, preposition, noun in jarr.
  2. Explain why الإنسان is accusative and العصر is genitive.
  3. Write two English translations — one literal, one natural — and note what the grammar emphasizes that a loose translation might miss.

Sample lesson · Qur'an K–2

Memorizing Surah Al-Ikhlas, verse by verse

Primary K–2 · Unit 2 · ~20 minutes
Objective: Learners recite the first two verses of Surah Al-Ikhlas from memory and can say in their own words that the surah is about Allah being One.

We begin with clean hands and the words a'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim, then the basmala. The teacher recites the first verse — قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ ("Say: He is Allah, the One") — clearly and slowly. The child repeats it three times. Then the second verse — اللَّهُ الصَّمَدُ ("Allah, the Eternal, the Absolute") — the same way. Finally the two are joined. We talk gently about what it means: there is only one Allah, and He needs nothing while everything needs Him.

Practice activity — "Echo & keep"
  • Teacher recites a verse; child echoes it three times, then says it alone.
  • Join verse 1 + verse 2 and recite them together twice.
  • Mark today's Qur'an habit in the tracker; recite again for a parent before bed.

Sample lesson · Qur'an 3–5

Getting the letters right: ح versus ه

Elementary 3–5 · Unit 2 · ~30 minutes
Objective: Learners produce the letters ح (haa) and ه (haa') from their correct points and read them accurately inside words from Surah Al-Fatihah.

Both letters sound like "h" to a new reader, but they come from different places. ح is made from the middle of the throat — a warm, breathy "H," like fogging a mirror on a cold day. ه is made from the deepest part of the throat — a softer, lighter "h." Getting these right matters: in الرَّحْمَٰن (Ar-Rahman) the letter is ح, while in اهْدِنَا (guide us) it is ه.

Practice activity — "Mirror & word hunt"
  1. Practice each sound alone five times, feeling where it starts in the throat.
  2. Read these Fatihah words aloud, naming which "h" each uses: الرَّحْمَٰن, الرَّحِيم, اهْدِنَا.
  3. Recite Al-Fatihah once, slowing down on every ح and ه, and have a parent check.

Sample lesson · Qur'an 6–8

Noon Sakinah, part 1: Idhar, Idgham, Iqlab, Ikhfa

Middle 6–8 · Unit 1 · ~45 minutes
Objective: Learners identify a noon sakinah (نْ) or tanween in the text and apply the correct one of the four rules based on the letter that follows.

A noon sakinah is a noon with a sukoon (نْ); a tanween is the doubled ending (ً ٍ ٌ) that sounds like a noon. What we do with that "n" sound depends entirely on the next letter:

The four rules
  • Idhar (make it clear): before a throat letter (ء ه ع ح غ خ) — pronounce the noon plainly. Example: مِنْ عِلْمٍ.
  • Idgham (merge): before ي ر م ل و ن — the noon melts into the next letter (with ghunnah for ينمو, without for ل ر). Example: مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ.
  • Iqlab (convert): before ب — the noon becomes a hidden meem with ghunnah. Example: مِنْ بَعْدِ.
  • Ikhfa (conceal): before the remaining 15 letters — a light nasal "n" held for two counts. Example: مِنْ قَبْلُ.
Practice activity — rule spotting
  1. In a short passage, circle every noon sakinah and tanween.
  2. Write which rule applies to each and why (name the following letter).
  3. Recite the passage applying each rule; a parent or teacher checks against the four rules. (This pairs with the "Tajweed: Rules of Noon Sakinah" lesson in Learn.)

Sample lesson · Qur'an 9–12

Tafsir of Surah Al-'Asr: three verses, a whole way of life

High School 9–12 · Unit 2 · ~50 minutes
Objective: Learners explain the meaning of Surah Al-'Asr, the significance of Allah swearing by time, and the four qualities that save a person from loss — and connect them to their own life.

Imam ash-Shafi'i said that if people reflected only on this surah, it would be enough to guide them. Allah swears وَالْعَصْرِ — "By time" — because time is the one resource every person spends and cannot recover. The verdict is sweeping: إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ لَفِي خُسْرٍ — "Indeed, mankind is in loss." Then comes the exception (istithna) that rescues us: except those who (1) believe, (2) do righteous deeds, (3) counsel one another to truth, and (4) counsel one another to patience. Faith and action are personal; the two counsels are social — a community that reminds one another.

Practice activity — reflect & apply
  1. Explain in writing why an oath by "time" fits a surah about loss.
  2. List the four saving qualities and give one concrete example of each from your week.
  3. Short reflection: how does "counseling to patience" show up at home or with friends? Recite the surah with full Tajweed to close.

Sample lesson · Islamic Studies K–2

The Five Pillars on one hand

Primary K–2 · Unit 3 · ~25 minutes
Objective: Learners name the five pillars of Islam in order and say, in a simple sentence, what each one is.

The five pillars are the five most important things a Muslim does — like five strong pillars that hold up a house. We count them on the five fingers of one hand: (1) Shahadah — saying and believing "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is His Messenger"; (2) Salah — praying five times a day; (3) Zakat — giving to those in need; (4) Sawm — fasting in the month of Ramadan; (5) Hajj — visiting the Ka'bah in Makkah once in a lifetime if we are able.

Practice activity — "Five fingers"
  • Trace your hand and label each finger with a pillar; color it in.
  • Touch each finger and say the pillar's name and one thing about it.
  • Which pillar do we do five times every day? Mark today's Salah in the habit tracker.

Wudu in order: preparing to stand before Allah

Elementary 3–5 · Unit 4 · ~35 minutes
Objective: Learners perform the steps of wudu in the correct order and explain why we make wudu before salah.

Before we pray, we clean ourselves with water — this is wudu. It prepares the body and the heart to stand before Allah. We begin by saying Bismillah and making the intention. The order is: wash the hands, rinse the mouth, rinse the nose, wash the face, wash the arms to the elbows (right then left), wipe the head, wipe the ears, and wash the feet to the ankles (right then left). Most parts are done three times.

Practice activity — "Order it & do it"
  1. Put picture cards of the wudu steps in the correct order.
  2. Perform wudu with a parent watching, saying Bismillah first.
  3. Answer: which side do we wash first, right or left? Why do we make wudu before we pray?

Fiqh of Salah: the pillars (arkan) versus the sunnahs

Middle 6–8 · Unit 2 · ~45 minutes
Objective: Learners distinguish the essential pillars of the prayer (which invalidate salah if left out) from the recommended sunnah acts, and explain sujud as-sahw.

Not every part of the prayer carries the same weight. The pillars (arkan) are essential — leaving one out means that part of the prayer must be repeated. They include standing (for those able), the opening takbir, reciting Al-Fatihah, ruku', sujud, sitting for the final tashahhud, and the closing salaam. The sunnahs are recommended acts the Prophet ﷺ did regularly — like raising the hands at the takbir or reciting a surah after Al-Fatihah — which bring reward but do not invalidate the prayer if missed. When someone forgets an obligatory (wajib) part, they perform sujud as-sahw — two prostrations to make up for forgetfulness.

Practice activity — sort & decide
  1. Sort a list of prayer actions into "pillar" and "sunnah."
  2. Case: someone forgets to sit for the first tashahhud and remembers after standing — what should they do?
  3. Explain in two sentences the difference between a rukn and a sunnah, and when sujud as-sahw is made.

Aqeedah: the three categories of Tawhid

High School 9–12 · Unit 1 · ~50 minutes
Objective: Learners define the three categories of tawhid, give an example of each, and explain why this framework matters for belief and worship.

Tawhid — affirming the Oneness of Allah — is the heart of Islamic creed. Scholars describe it in three connected categories. Tawhid ar-Rububiyyah (Lordship): Allah alone creates, sustains, and controls all things. Tawhid al-Uluhiyyah (Worship): therefore Allah alone deserves our worship — prayer, du'a, sacrifice, hope, and fear are directed to Him. Tawhid al-Asma' wa-s-Sifat (Names & Attributes): we affirm the beautiful names and attributes Allah affirmed for Himself in the Qur'an and Sunnah, without distortion, denial, or likening Him to creation (لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ — "There is nothing like unto Him"). The key insight: even those who accept Allah as Creator (rububiyyah) have not fulfilled tawhid until they worship Him alone (uluhiyyah).

Practice activity — categorize & defend
  1. Match statements to the correct category (e.g., "Allah gives life and death" → Rububiyyah; "We pray only to Allah" → Uluhiyyah; "Ar-Rahman is one of His names" → Asma' wa Sifat).
  2. Cite one Qur'anic verse that supports each category.
  3. Short essay: why did the Qur'an argue from rububiyyah (which most people accepted) toward uluhiyyah (worshiping Allah alone)?

How mastery is recorded

Younger learners are assessed with standards-based checkpoints (Beginning · Developing · Secure) so growth is visible without grades. From Middle school up, work earns letter grades and course credit that flow into the Gradebook and, for high school, the transcript in Compliance. Qur'an memorization and recitation are logged separately with a review cycle, and character habits are tracked daily rather than graded. Objectives are written as clear skills so families can align them to their state's record-keeping. Note: this curriculum is faith-integrated and is offered in addition to the state's required subjects, not in place of them.
