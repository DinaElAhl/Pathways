English Language Arts

From first sounds to skilled rhetoric — reading, writing, speaking, and listening with clarity, imagination, and good character. Grades K–12.
Open in Learn

The through-line: sound → word → sentence → story → argument

Learners begin by hearing and forming the sounds of language, grow into fluent readers and confident writers, then move on to analyze great literature and craft persuasive, well-researched prose. Every level pairs skill-building with worthy reading that quietly models honesty, kindness, courage, and gratitude.

What this subject builds toward

Reading

Decoding and fluency in the early years mature into comprehension strategies, literary analysis, and independent critical reading of full-length classics.

Writing

From letter formation and complete sentences to structured paragraphs, multi-paragraph essays, research papers with citations, and persuasive rhetoric.

Language & Speaking

Phonemic awareness, vocabulary, grammar mastery, and word roots; plus recitation, discussion, and oral presentation at every band.

Faith & character, woven in naturally. Reading selections and writing prompts often invite reflection on honesty, patience, mercy, gratitude, and courage — the same virtues families care about — without ever becoming preachy. Literature is chosen to be wholesome and worthy of discussion around the kitchen table.
Standards note. Units are aligned to Common Core–style English Language Arts objectives for reading (literature & informational text), writing, speaking & listening, and language conventions. Objectives are described for planning and portfolio use; Pathways is an independent curriculum and does not claim official state or Common Core endorsement.

Sample lessons
Sample Lesson · Grade 1 · Phonics

Objective

Blend three sounds to read and spell short-a CVC words (consonant–vowel–consonant), such as cat, map, sad, ran.

Lesson

We warm up by "robot talking": the teacher says /m/ … /a/ … /p/ slowly and the learner pushes the sounds together into map. Then we reverse it — hear the whole word and tap out each sound on three fingers.

Using letter tiles a–m–p–c–t–s, we build a word, swap one tile, and read the new word aloud. Changing the first sound turns cat into sat, then mat. This shows that letters carry sounds, and sounds make words.

Practice — Word-family ladder
  1. Start with cat. Read it aloud, tapping each sound.
  2. Change just the first letter to make sat, rat, bat.
  3. Now change the last letter of cat to make can, cap, cab.
  4. Write your two favorite words in your notebook and draw a tiny picture for each.
Sample Lesson · Grade 2 · Sentences

Objective

Write a complete sentence with a capital letter, an end mark, and both a naming part (who or what) and a telling part (what they do).

Lesson

Every sentence answers two questions: Who or what? and What happens? In "The bird sings," the bird is the naming part and sings is the telling part. Take one away and the thought feels unfinished — "The bird" leaves us waiting.

We fix "grumpy fragments" together. Given "ran to the door," we ask who ran? and add a naming part: "The puppy ran to the door." Then we check the capital letter at the front and the period at the end.

Practice — Build & fix
  • Fix these fragments into full sentences: "jumped over the log" · "my kind sister" · "the warm bread".
  • Write one sentence about something kind you did today. Circle the naming part; underline the telling part.
  • Check: capital at the start? end mark at the finish?

Sample lessons
Sample Lesson · Grade 4 · Comprehension

Objective

Make an inference by combining clues in the text with what you already know, and support it with evidence.

Lesson

Authors don't tell us everything — good readers "read between the lines." An inference is a smart guess: text clue + my knowledge = inference. If a story says "Maryam pulled her coat tight and her breath made little clouds," the author never wrote "it was cold," yet we know it was.

We practice on a short passage about a boy who "kept glancing at the clock and couldn't finish his breakfast." Clues (watching the clock, no appetite) plus what we know (people feel this way before something big) let us infer he was nervous or excited about the day ahead — then we point back to the exact words that told us.

Practice — Clue + Knowledge = Inference
  1. Read three short scenes provided in the lesson.
  2. For each, fill a three-column chart: the text clue, what I know, and my inference.
  3. Write one sentence: "I can tell that ___ because the text says ___."
Sample Lesson · Grade 5 · Writing

Objective

Plan and draft a strong body paragraph with a clear topic sentence and at least three supporting details.

Lesson

Think of a paragraph as a table: the topic sentence is the tabletop (the one big idea), and the supporting details are the legs that hold it up. A wobbly paragraph has too few legs, or legs that belong to a different table.

Together we take the topic sentence "Helping at home teaches responsibility." Then we brainstorm legs: you learn to finish what you start; family members can rely on you; you feel proud of a job done well. We arrange them from smallest to strongest and add a closing sentence that echoes the big idea.

Practice — Table-top & legs
  • Choose a topic sentence: "A good friend can be trusted" or "Reading before bed is a good habit."
  • Draw a table and write three "legs" (details) that support it.
  • Write the full paragraph: topic sentence, three details, closing sentence. Reread and fix one thing.

Sample lessons
Sample Lesson · Grade 7 · Literary Analysis

Objective

Support an interpretation of a character using the quote-and-explain method: cite text evidence, then explain how it proves your point.

Lesson

A claim without evidence is just an opinion; evidence without explanation is just a quote. Strong analysis links the two. The pattern is simple: make a point → prove it with a quote → explain how the quote proves it.

Suppose we claim a character is generous. We hunt the text for a moment that shows it — perhaps he "handed the last of his bread to the shivering traveler without a word." We quote it exactly, then explain: giving away his last food, and doing it quietly, shows generosity that expects nothing back. The explanation is where the thinking happens.

Practice — Point · Evidence · Explanation
  1. Choose one trait for a character from your current reading (brave, stubborn, loyal…).
  2. Find one quotation that shows it. Copy it exactly, with the page number.
  3. Write a P-E-E paragraph: state the trait, give the quote, then explain in 2–3 sentences how the quote proves it.
Sample Lesson · Grade 8 · Argument Writing

Objective

Strengthen a persuasive essay by addressing a counterargument and then refuting it.

Lesson

The most convincing writers don't pretend the other side doesn't exist — they face it head-on. Naming the strongest objection to your claim (the counterargument) shows fairness and makes your own position sturdier when you answer it (the refutation).

Take the claim "Middle schoolers should have daily reading time." A thoughtful opponent might say, "There isn't enough time in the school day." We acknowledge that honestly, then refute: even fifteen minutes replaces low-value filler, and the gains in vocabulary and focus save time elsewhere. Words like Some argue… however… the evidence shows… signal the turn.

Practice — Steel-man & refute
  • Write your claim in one sentence.
  • Write the strongest objection an honest opponent could make (steel-man it, don't weaken it).
  • Write a refutation paragraph beginning "Some may argue…" and turning with "However…" backed by a reason or fact.

Sample lessons
Sample Lesson · English 11 · American Literature

Objective

Analyze how the Transcendentalists advanced a central idea, using a passage from Thoreau's Walden, and write a focused analytical paragraph.

Lesson

When Thoreau writes, "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately… and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived," he compresses the whole Transcendentalist project into a sentence: strip life to its essentials and live it with intention. We read the passage twice — once for sense, once for craft.

Notice the diction of purpose (deliberately, front only the essential facts) and the stark contrast between living and merely existing. We connect it to Emerson's call for self-reliance and ask what "living deliberately" might cost — and what it might be worth. Analysis means naming a technique and explaining its effect on meaning, not just paraphrasing.

Practice — Analytical paragraph
  1. Choose one sentence from the excerpt that best captures Thoreau's message.
  2. Identify one technique he uses (diction, contrast, metaphor, repetition).
  3. Write a paragraph: state the idea, quote the line, name the technique, and explain how it deepens the meaning. End by connecting it to a modern choice a reader might face.
Sample Lesson · English 9 · Composition

Objective

Craft an arguable thesis statement that takes a defensible position rather than stating an obvious fact.

Lesson

A thesis is the spine of an essay — everything else stands on it. The test of a good thesis is simple: could a reasonable person disagree? "The Odyssey is an epic poem" fails the test (no one disagrees). "Odysseus's cleverness matters more to his survival than his strength" passes — it stakes a claim you must then defend.

We revise weak theses together. A vague "This essay is about honesty in the story" becomes "The character's small lie unravels precisely because honesty, once broken, is nearly impossible to rebuild." Now the essay has a direction, a claim, and a promise to the reader.

Practice — Fact vs. claim
  • Sort five given statements into "fact" (not arguable) and "claim" (arguable).
  • Take two of the facts and rewrite each into an arguable thesis.
  • Draft your own thesis for a text you're reading, then apply the test: could someone reasonably disagree? Revise until yes.

Materials & assessments

What a learner actually does in a typical week of English Language Arts.

Lessons

Short guided lessons with read-alouds or model texts, taught in Learn or read together — one core skill at a time.

Practice

Daily reading, spelling/vocabulary work, grammar exercises, and writing in a journal or draft — the reps that build fluency.

Quizzes

Auto-graded checks on phonics, vocabulary, grammar, and comprehension, plus spelling assessments — quick feedback for parents.

Projects

Book reports, essays, poems, research papers, and presentations — kept in the portfolio for evaluations and transcripts.

Portfolio & grading. Writing projects and reading responses are saved to each child's portfolio for Pennsylvania's annual evaluator review. Younger learners are assessed against reading/writing standards (developing → secure); older learners earn letter grades and, in high school, full English credits toward the transcript.
